# Executive Decision Brief: Bounded Autonomy

## The decision in plain language

Do not decide whether “the agent” is approved. Decide which specific work it may
perceive, recommend, decide, and do; whose authority it uses; what limits apply;
and what evidence could justify keeping, narrowing, or expanding that scope.

## What this can unlock

- automation that can adapt within governed paths instead of only following a
  fixed script;
- shorter time from information to useful action;
- supervised delegation of repetitive work while preserving scarce judgment;
- evidence-based expansion instead of an all-or-nothing autonomy bet.

## What it protects

It limits the chance that an impressive interface conceals broad permissions,
unbounded effects, stale memory, abandoned obligations, or missing evidence. It
does not prove that an agent is safe or that the business outcome will improve.

## Questions before delegation

1. What useful work are we delegating, and to whom is value delivered?
2. Whose authority does each action use, and where is it enforced?
3. What powers remain withheld?
4. What limits accumulated consequences across retries, tools, events, and time?
5. Who owns work after a model session ends?
6. What stops, revokes, contains, reconstructs, and corrects action?
7. What evidence changes the autonomy level?

## Evidence packet

Require an authority map, governed tool contract, action budget, memory and
provenance record, incident path, supervised observations, residual exposure,
and an accountable expansion decision.

## Decision options

Observe only, advise, recommend for approval, act within one bounded scope,
expand a previously evidenced scope, constrain, or stop. Approval should expire
or be reconsidered when assumptions, tools, data, or consequences change.
